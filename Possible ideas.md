# Possible Ideas

## 1. Treaty of Guadalupe Hidalgo (1848): the founding case

Clause: Article IX. The Senate changed the promise of citizenship to "at the proper time (to be judged of by the Congress)."
Why it matters: One small edit turned a promise with a timeline into one Congress could delay indefinitely. Its effects on land ownership are recorded in decades of claims cases.
Needs: A reader of 1840s legal Spanish.
Verdict: Strongest fit. Already the year-one case.

## 2. Adams-Onís Treaty (1819): Spain cedes Florida

Clause: Article 8. Spanish land grants "shall be ratified and confirmed."
Why it matters: The US Supreme Court read the English text one way in Foster v. Neilson (1829). After examining the Spanish text, it reversed itself in US v. Percheman (1833). A court changed its ruling because of a translation difference.
Needs: The same Spanish skills as case 1.
Verdict: Best second case.

## 3. Gadsden Purchase (1853): southern Arizona and New Mexico

Clause: Articles V–VI. These carry forward Guadalupe's protections but narrow which land grants count.
Why it matters: Same two countries and same languages, five years after Guadalupe. It works as a direct follow-up check.
Needs: The same Spanish skills. The treaty is short.
Verdict: Easiest entry point. Good for newer members.

## 4. Treaty of Paris (1898): Puerto Rico, the Philippines, Guam

Clause: Article IX. The inhabitants' political status "shall be determined by the Congress."
Why it matters: The promise of citizenship disappears entirely, and the Insular Cases litigated the results.
Needs: Spanish skills. The founding document already names a Bancroft source.
Verdict: Strong. It is the endpoint of the lineage below.

The lineage option: cases 1–4 studied together
The same "inhabitants of ceded land" clause appears in 1803, 1819, 1848, and 1898. The research question is whether the promise gets steadily weaker over time. This is the most ambitious option and the most publishable, because it tests a pattern instead of a single event.

Possible, but needs outside help

## 5. Louisiana Purchase (1803)

Clause: Article III. Inhabitants are admitted "as soon as possible."
Needs: French.
Verdict: Best used as the starting point of the lineage, coding the English text only.

## 6. Treaty of Wuchale (1889): Italy and Ethiopia

Clause: Article 17. The Amharic text says Ethiopia may use Italy for foreign affairs. Italy read its own text as saying Ethiopia must, and the dispute led to war in 1896.
Needs: An Amharic reader. Historians also debate how clear-cut this difference really is, so it would need verification.
Verdict: Useful as a test of whether the codebook catches "may" vs. "must."

## 7. Treaty of Waitangi (1840): Britain and the Māori

Clause: The English text says the Māori gave up "sovereignty"; the Māori text says "governance." A tribunal has spent decades ruling on this difference.
Needs: A te reo Māori scholar as a partner, plus care around Māori data rights.
Verdict: An excellent match on paper, but blocked without that partner.

## 8. Treaty of Versailles (1919)

Clause: Article 231, the "war guilt" clause.
Why it's limited: Only the English and French texts were official. The famous dispute came from the German translation, and it concerns tone ("guilt" vs. "liability"), not obligation. The current codebook cannot detect that kind of difference.
Verdict: Only useful as a test of the codebook's limits, not as a main case.
Not recommended

## 9. Geneva Conference (1954)
The key document was never signed by anyone, so the question is its legal standing, not its wording. It would also require French and Vietnamese.

## 10. UN Resolution 242 (1967)
The English "territories" vs. French "des territoires" difference is a good one-line example for a write-up. As a club case it is too short and too politically charged.

## 11. US–Native American treaties
These were written only in English. The real differences lay between the written text and what interpreters said aloud, and there is no second text to compare.
